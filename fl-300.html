FORM FL-300 · JUDICIAL COUNCIL OF CALIFORNIA

Request for Order

The form used to ask a family court judge to step in mid-case — for custody, support, attorney's fees, or other relief before the final judgment.

What this form actually does

Once a family law case is open, an FL-300 (often called an "RFO") is how either party asks the court to make a specific order before the case is fully resolved — temporary custody or visitation, temporary child or spousal support, attorney's fees, or changes to existing orders. It's essentially a motion: you're asking the judge to act now rather than waiting for trial or final judgment.

Before you file

  • Identify exactly what you're asking for. FL-300 covers many types of relief — be specific about which orders you want rather than checking every box that seems related.
  • Gather supporting evidence. You'll typically need a supporting declaration (your sworn written statement of facts) and, for support requests, a current FL-150.
  • Check the hearing calendar and required notice. Courts set a hearing date when you file, and the other side must be served with proper notice before that date — timelines vary by county and by whether you're requesting emergency (ex parte) relief.

Filling it out, section by section

  1. Case information. Match the existing case number and party names exactly.
  2. Type of relief requested. Check the specific boxes for what you want: child custody/visitation, child support, spousal support, attorney's fees, property control, domestic violence-related orders, or other relief.
  3. Attach a declaration. Explain in your own words, under penalty of perjury, why the court should grant what you're asking for — facts, not arguments.
  4. Attach required companion forms. For support requests, attach a current FL-150. For custody requests, some counties require additional local forms — check before filing.
  5. File and arrange service. File with the clerk to get a hearing date, then have the other party properly served with the filed papers and enough notice before the hearing date.
Emergency requests are different: if you need an order before the normal hearing process could happen (like an urgent safety issue), that's an ex parte request — a separate, faster process with its own forms and much shorter notice requirements. An ordinary FL-300 is not the right vehicle for a same-day emergency; ask the court self-help center about ex parte procedure if your situation is urgent.

Common mistakes

  • Checking boxes for relief not actually supported by the attached declaration.
  • Missing the required notice period before the hearing, which can get the hearing continued.
  • Requesting support without attaching a current FL-150.
  • Treating a routine RFO as the path for a genuine emergency, when ex parte procedure exists for that purpose.

Filing snapshot

Who files it
Either party in an open family law case.
When
Any time during the case when a party needs a court order before final judgment.
Where
Filed in the same family law case and department.
Filing fee
Varies by county and request type; some motions have separate fees.
Pairs well with
FL-150 — Income and Expense Declaration
